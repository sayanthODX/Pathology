Leukocyte Activation & Phagocytosis

This section introduces the foundational concepts of phagocytosis based on Elie Metchnikoff's initial findings. When recruited leukocytes (mainly neutrophils and macrophages) encounter microbes or dead cells, they undergo activation. The most critical functional response is phagocytosis—the sequential process of ingesting and destroying particulate material. Interact with the key steps below to understand the overarching sequence before diving into biochemical specifics.

The 3 Key Steps of Phagocytosis

Click each sequential phase to explore its physiological mechanisms.

Recognition and Attachment

Leukocytes must accurately identify the injurious agent before ingestion. They express specialized surface receptors that detect microbial molecular patterns (like TLRs and Mannose receptors) or host proteins called opsonins that coat the microbe (like IgG and C3b).

Engulfment

Following attachment, actin polymerization drives the extension of cellular pseudopods around the microbe. These pseudopods fuse to form an intracellular vacuole called a phagosome, which subsequently fuses with lysosomes to form the phagolysosome.

Killing and Degradation

Within the acidic environment of the phagolysosome, the ingested material is destroyed by highly reactive oxygen species (ROS), reactive nitrogen species (RNS), and potent lysosomal enzymes (like myeloperoxidase and acid hydrolases).

⚠

Clinical Pathology Highlight: Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency (LAD)

An autosomal recessive disorder characterized by defects in leukocyte adhesion molecules. Neutrophils cannot exit the circulation to reach the site of infection. Hallmark signs: Marked leukocytosis (high WBC count in blood) and increased susceptibility to severe infections.

Receptors & Opsonization

Phagocytes rely on specific receptors to distinguish foreign material from healthy self-tissue. This section breaks down the primary phagocytic receptors and explores Opsonins—host proteins that coat microbes to massively amplify phagocytic efficiency. Review the table to map specific opsonins to their corresponding leukocyte receptors.

Primary Phagocytic Receptors

Toll-like Receptors (TLRs)

Detect specific microbial molecular products.

GPCRs

Recognize bacterial N-formyl methionine residues.

Mannose Receptors

Bind terminal mannose/fucose on microbial cell walls.

Scavenger Receptors

Ingest modified LDL particles and a variety of microbes.

Clinical Correlate: Bruton Disease

A genetic defect in B-cell maturation leading to absent immunoglobulin (antibody) production. Consequently, there is defective opsonization, leaving the patient highly susceptible to infections.

Engulfment Mechanics

Engulfment is an active physical process driven by cytoskeletal rearrangements. The cell membrane flows around the opsonized particle to internalize it. The subsequent fusion with lysosomes is crucial for exposing the microbe to destructive enzymes. Review the physical steps below, followed by a critical genetic defect that interrupts this process.

↻

1. Actin Polymerization

Receptor binding triggers rapid actin filament polymerization, pushing the plasma membrane to form extensions called pseudopods.

◯

2. Phagosome Formation

Pseudopods "zipper" around the microbe and pinch off from the membrane, forming an intracellular vacuole called the phagosome.

✽

3. Phagolysosome Fusion

The phagosome fuses with cytoplasmic lysosomes. Granules discharge hydrolytic enzymes into the new phagolysosome, activated by acidic pH.

⚠

High-Yield Clinical: Chédiak-Higashi Syndrome

An autosomal recessive condition characterized by severe defects in phagolysosome function.

  • ✖ Mutation: LYST gene (Lysosomal Trafficking Regulator).
  • ✖ Mechanism: Defective fusion of phagosomes with lysosomes.
  • ✖ Result: Delayed microbial killing, neutropenia, defective degranulation.

Pathognomonic Finding

Giant granules in leukocytes observable on a peripheral blood smear. These occur due to aberrant, premature fusion of lysosomal granules during leukocyte maturation. Bleeding disorders also occur due to defects in platelet dense granules.

Reactivity of ROS

Not all Reactive Oxygen Species are equally destructive. Superoxide and Hydrogen Peroxide are relatively weak intermediates. The ultimate goal of the respiratory burst is to convert them into highly toxic endpoints like Hypochlorite and Hydroxyl radicals.

Nonoxidative Mechanisms (Leukocyte Granules)

While ROS and RNS are extremely potent, leukocytes also utilize oxygen-independent methods. Lysosomal acid hydrolases digest dead microbes within the acidic environment of the phagolysosome, while various granule proteins (proteases, antimicrobial peptides) degrade bacterial structures directly.
